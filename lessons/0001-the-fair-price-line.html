Lesson 0001 · Swing Trading with VWAP

The Fair Price Line

One skill: look at a daily chart with an anchored VWAP and say, in one sentence, who has controlled the move since the anchor — and why you're entitled to say it.

What the line is

VWAP is the average price actually paid per share since some starting point, with every trade weighted by its size: 

VWAP = Σ (price × volume) / Σ (volume)     — cumulative from the anchor

A day that closes at $50.10 on 40 million shares moves it far more than one at $50.40 on 2 million. That weighting is the whole point: a simple moving average tells you where price has been; VWAP tells you what the crowd has paid (StockCharts: formula and worked example).

Day traders start the sum at today's open ("session VWAP"), which resets every morning — useless for a hold measured in weeks. Swing traders instead anchor the sum at a meaningful event — an earnings gap, a breakout day, a major swing low — producing an anchored VWAP (AVWAP): the average price of everyone who has traded since that event (StockCharts: Anchored VWAP). Everything in this course is anchored.

Why it isn't just another squiggle

Institutional trading desks are graded against VWAP: a buy program filled below it "beat the benchmark," one filled above it didn't (Madhavan, "VWAP Strategies," 2002). Big positions aren't built in a day — an institution accumulating for a week after earnings is running VWAP-benchmarked orders day after day (Busseti & Boyd), so real multi-day order flow is anchored to the line. No moving average has that property — nobody's bonus depends on the 20-EMA.

The control read

Since the AVWAP is what the average participant has paid since the event, price's position against it says who is winning the move:

  • Price holding above a rising AVWAP — the average participant since the event is in profit, dips keep getting bought. Buyers control the move; pullbacks toward the line are where they tend to defend. This is the read behind Shannon's core swing framework (Alphatrends VWAP archive).
  • Price holding below a falling AVWAP — the average participant since the event is underwater, rallies get sold into (trapped buyers exiting at breakeven). Sellers control the move.
  • Price crossing a flat AVWAP repeatedly — no one is in control since that anchor. This is a real answer, and the discipline to say it is most of the skill.
The Grimes caveat No free quantitative study shows a statistical edge in VWAP touches by themselves — Adam Grimes tested it and found none, and even VWAP-heavy prop desks say outright you cannot trade solely on it. The control read is context: it tells you which trades are allowed, not when to click. Keep this caveat for the whole course.

Check yourself

Answer from memory — one attempt each, feedback is immediate.

Since its earnings gap three weeks ago, a stock has held above its earnings-anchored VWAP, and the line slopes upward. The one-sentence read is…

Above a rising AVWAP, the average participant since the event is in profit and dips are being bought — the definition of buyers controlling the move.

The average participant since the anchor event is sitting on a loss whenever…

The AVWAP is the average price paid since the event. Price below it means the average buyer is underwater — the engine behind rallies getting sold in a broken move.

Institutional order flow clusters around VWAP because…

VWAP is the dominant execution benchmark (Madhavan 2002). Multi-day accumulation programs are judged against it, so large orders are scheduled around it — that grading, not chart magic, anchors the flow.

A month after your anchor event, the AVWAP is flat and price has crossed it six times. The correct read is…

Repeated crosses of a flat AVWAP mean no one owns the move since that anchor. "No control, no trade" is a complete and correct read — saying it out loud is the skill.
